# A Modular Blueprint for Near-Zero-Failure Tajweed Detection: Leveraging Fine-Tuned ASR and Forced Alignment for Articulation-Level Error Checking

## Foundational Datasets for Continuous-Speech Quranic Analysis

The development of a reliable, low-false-positive Tajweed error detection system hinges critically on the availability of high-quality, appropriately annotated datasets. The user's primary requirement is for resources capable of supporting analysis at the phoneme-, letter-, or articulation-point (makhraj/sifat) level within continuous, melismatic recitation. An exhaustive review of the provided materials reveals a clear hierarchy of dataset utility, where one resource stands out as foundational while others serve important but more limited roles. A significant finding is the absence of any publicly available, large-scale dataset that explicitly provides time-aligned makhraj or sifat labels for continuous speech [[3](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/132991), [28](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/iel8/6287639/10380310/10589392.pdf)]. This gap necessitates a strategic pivot towards leveraging datasets with high-quality word and phoneme alignments as the bedrock for a two-step process involving ASR followed by forced alignment and phonetic conversion.

The most prominent and valuable resource identified is the **Quran-MD dataset**, which is repeatedly referenced as a comprehensive, multimodal corpus [[136](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/133014), [177](https://huggingface.co/papers/2601.17880), [183](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17880)]. This dataset integrates textual, linguistic, and audio dimensions at both the verse and word levels, making it uniquely suited for the task at hand [[136](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/133014)]. Its key advantage lies in its provision of high-quality, time-aligned annotations across thousands of Qur'anic verses, covering multiple canonical reciters [[87](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.17880), [136](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/133014)]. This granularity is essential for training and evaluating the alignment components of a proposed pipeline. While Quran-MD provides the crucial word and phoneme timestamps, it does not contain explicit makhraj or sifat labels, a limitation that reinforces the need for a phonetic decoder to bridge this gap. Nonetheless, its existence represents a major step forward, providing the necessary ground truth for aligning transcripts with audio in a manner that respects the structure of continuous recitation.

Another highly relevant large-scale resource is the **Tadabur dataset**, which comprises over 1400 hours of recitation audio from more than 600 distinct reciters [[24](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.18932v1), [88](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.18932)]. Its sheer scale and diversity make it an invaluable asset for fine-tuning ASR models to handle the vocal variations among different qaris within the Hafs 'an Asim tradition, such as Al-Husary, Al-Afasi, and As-Sudais [[88](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.18932)]. Although the precise alignment granularity of Tadabur is less detailed than that of Quran-MD, its volume of clean, naturalistic speech provides an excellent foundation for training robust acoustic models. The combination of Tadabur's breadth and Quran-MD's depth offers a powerful dual-pronged strategy for model development.

In contrast to these large-scale corpora, the **QDAT dataset** serves a more specialized purpose [[137](https://ijasat.com/index.php/ijasat_en/article/view/86), [139](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/404012371_QDAT_A_data_set_for_Reciting_the_Quran)]. It consists of crowdsourced audio recordings, primarily from Surah Al-Maidah verse 109, manually annotated by experts to indicate correctness or incorrectness according to three specific Tajweed rules: 'Separate stretching' (Al Mad), 'Tight Noon' (Ghunnah), and 'Hide' (Ikhfaa) [[123](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06429), [137](https://ijasat.com/index.php/ijasat_en/article/view/86)]. This dataset has been used as a benchmark for classifier-based approaches, with studies achieving accuracies of 96% for Al Mad, 95% for Ghunnah, and 96% for Ikhfaa using Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks with Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficient (MFCC) features [[123](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06429)]. More recent work using an EfficientNet-B0 model reported improved accuracy of 99.34% for Ghunnah, demonstrating the dataset's continued relevance for developing targeted mispronunciation classifiers [[161](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.23470v2)]. While QDAT cannot support a full phoneme-level decoding pipeline due to its narrow focus and lack of transcript alignment, it remains a critical resource for validating and potentially augmenting a rule-specific component of a larger system.

Other datasets mentioned in the literature offer supplementary value. The **Ar-DAD** corpus is noted for its focus on improving automatic forced alignment for phonemes in Qur'anic contexts, suggesting it may contain relevant aligned data, though its accessibility requires further verification [[1](http://irep.iium.edu.my/111820/2/111820_Improving%20automatic%20forced%20alignment.pdf)]. The **Muharaf dataset**, created using the ScribeArabic annotation tool, also contains structured data for Quranic research [[170](https://github.com/mehreenmehreen/ScribeArabic)]. Additionally, curated collections like `quran-audio-text-dataset` and `quran-tajweed` provide access to complete Quran recitations with granular annotations and Tajweed rule highlighting, respectively, which could be useful for smaller-scale experiments or as part of a broader data acquisition effort [[38](https://github.com/cpfair/quran-tajweed), [70](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Buraaq/quran-audio-text-dataset)].

The QDAT dataset can then be used to develop and test specialized modules for specific Tajweed rules, feeding back into the main pipeline. The lack of explicit makhraj/sifat labeled data means that any solution must rely on a phonetic transcription step to infer articulatory information indirectly, a topic explored in subsequent sections.

## Models and Pipelines for Continuous-Speech Phonetic Decoding

Given the challenges of general-purpose models and the absence of a single end-to-end solution for Tajweed-aware decoding, the most pragmatic approach involves constructing a modular pipeline composed of several specialized, open-weight models. This architecture, which aligns with the user's preference for a custom rule engine, centers on first generating a high-fidelity transcript of continuous speech and then precisely aligning that transcript with the audio to produce a time-stamped phonetic sequence. The leading candidates for the initial ASR stage are variants of the Whisper model, which have demonstrated strong performance on Arabic speech and have active communities working on their fine-tuning for Quranic recitation. These base models can then be further adapted using large-scale datasets like Tadabur to enhance their ability to handle the unique prosodic and phonetic demands of melismatic Tajweed-style recitation [[53](https://www.oajaiml.com/uploads/archivepdf/194262291.pdf), [63](https://aclanthology.org/2025.arabicnlp-sharedtasks.66.pdf)].

OpenAI's Whisper series of models serves as a powerful starting point due to its state-of-the-art performance and self-hostability [[50](https://huggingface.co/tarteel-ai/whisper-base-ar-quran)]. Several projects on platforms like Hugging Face demonstrate successful fine-tuning of Whisper for the specific domain of Quranic Arabic. For instance, `habib-hf/tarbiyah-ai-v1-1` is a fine-tuned version of `whisper-small`, and `tarteel-ai/whisper-base-ar-quran` is another variant optimized for the task [[50](https://huggingface.co/tarteel-ai/whisper-base-ar-quran), [84](https://huggingface.co/Habib-HF/tarbiyah-ai-v1-1)]. Similarly, `yousifgamalo/quran-s-finetuned` showcases a fine-tuned `whisper-medium` model [[143](https://huggingface.co/yousifgamalo/quran-s-finetuned)]. These examples provide concrete blueprints and pre-trained checkpoints that can significantly reduce the development effort. Furthermore, some efforts leverage techniques like Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to efficiently fine-tune large models like `whisper-m-quran-lora-dataset-mix` on diacritized recitations, showcasing advanced methods for domain adaptation [[86](https://huggingface.co/MaddoggProduction/whisper-m-quran-lora-dataset-mix)]. The core strategy would involve taking one of these existing fine-tuned models and performing additional fine-tuning on a large, continuous-speech corpus such as Tadabur to maximize its accuracy on the target domain [[88](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.18932)].

Once a continuous-speech ASR model generates a transcript, the next critical step is high-fidelity forced alignment. This process matches the words in the transcript to their corresponding segments in the original audio file, yielding precise start and end times. The **Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA)** is the de facto industry standard for this task and is widely cited in relevant academic literature [[228](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.18466v1), [229](https://aclanthology.org/2025.computel-main.11.pdf)]. Its release in 2016 established it as a foundational tool in phonetic research, and its flexibility allows it to be configured with custom pronunciation dictionaries [[228](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.18466v1)]. This feature is particularly important for the current goal, as it enables the inclusion of Tajweed-specific phonemes or symbols, ensuring the alignment process accounts for the unique sounds present in Qur'anic recitation. The quality of the MFA alignment is paramount, and its performance should be rigorously evaluated against gold-standard data like Quran-MD using the MFA's built-in evaluation tools [[230](https://montreal-forced-aligner.readthedocs.io/en/v3.3.3/user_guide/implementations/alignment_evaluation.html)]. Studies comparing modern ASR methods for forced alignment suggest that contemporary deep learning models integrated into alignment frameworks can outperform older Hidden Markov Model (HMM)-based approaches, reinforcing the suitability of a pipeline built around a powerful ASR model like Whisper [[226](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.19363v1)].

The final stage in this decoding pipeline is the generation of a rich, Tajweed-aware phonetic representation. Since no dataset provides explicit makhraj or sifat labels, this requires a dedicated phonemization step. The **Hetchy/Quranic-Phonemizer** project is explicitly designed for this purpose, aiming to create phonetic transcriptions and training data for machine learning systems with Tajweed awareness [[82](https://github.com/Hetchy/Quranic-Phonemizer)]. Another compelling concept is the **Quran Phonetic Script (QPS)**, introduced in several academic papers as a method to encode Tajweed rules directly into the phonemic alphabet itself [[8](https://huggingface.co/papers/2509.00094), [56](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/80144)]. By applying a tool like the Quranic-Phonemizer or a custom implementation of the QPS logic to the words produced by the ASR model, one can convert them into a detailed phonetic script before the forced alignment step. This enriched phonetic representation would then be aligned by the MFA, resulting in a time-series output of Tajweed-aware phonemes. This entire pipeline—Whisper-based ASR, phonemization, and MFA alignment—transforms raw audio into the precise, articulated signal required by a custom rule engine, effectively bridging the gap left by the lack of direct makhraj/sifat labeled data.

## Commercial Approaches and Their Technical Implications

While the focus of this research is on open-weight solutions, analyzing the approaches taken by commercial applications like Tarteel.ai provides valuable context and validation. These companies operate in the same challenging space and their public-facing descriptions, combined with technical blog posts, offer clues about viable architectural pathways and the feasibility of the overall goal. The success of these commercial entities suggests that robust, real-time Tajweed feedback is achievable, even if their specific implementations remain proprietary. They confirm that a modular architecture combining ASR with sophisticated post-processing is a dominant paradigm in the field.

**Tarteel.ai** positions itself as "the world's first AI-powered Quran application" and a leader in AI-driven Quran memorization and revision [[15](https://tarteel.ai/blog/tarteels-ml-journey-part-1-intro-data-collection/amp/), [132](https://tarteel.ai/unwrapped/2024/global), [144](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YuEPGBePq3M)]. Its core functionality is described as using ASR to listen to a user's recitation and provide real-time feedback, highlighting mistakes and even vibrating to alert the user to errors [[21](https://tarteel.ai/blog/introducing-mistake-detection/amp/), [93](https://tarteel.ai/blog/introducing-mistake-detection/), [166](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.mmmoussa.iqra&hl=en_US)]. Critically for this research, Tarteel's developers have explicitly stated that the app currently only supports the Hafs 'an Asim recitation style, confirming that their engineering efforts are focused on mastering a single, canonical riwayah [[47](https://support.tarteel.ai/en/articles/12414396-does-tarteel-support-different-quran-riwayat-qira-at)]. This singular focus likely simplifies their modeling challenge compared to systems attempting to support multiple qira'at. In their technical blogs, Tarteel discusses their journey with self-supervised learning methods for ASR and the challenges of closing the gap between recitation and instruction [[15](https://tarteel.ai/blog/tarteels-ml-journey-part-1-intro-data-collection/amp/), [16](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/case-studies/automating-real-time-arabic-speech-recognition/)]. They detail their data collection and annotation processes, emphasizing the creation of tailored tools and workflows, which implies the development of a proprietary, high-quality dataset over time [[61](https://tarteel.ai/blog/tarteels-ml-journey-part-2/), [125](https://tarteel.ai/blog/tarteels-ml-journey-part-1-intro-data-collection/)]. The fact that they have successfully deployed a system that users find helpful indicates that a low-false-positive rate on correct recitations is a solvable engineering problem, achieved through careful model selection and tuning [[131](https://www.reddit.com/r/Hifdh/comments/1kalqui/tarteel_ai/)].

Another notable commercial player is **Qari AI**, which makes a specific and technologically distinct claim. Unlike the more generic description of Tarteel's ASR-based approach, Qari AI states that it "explicitly models where each letter originates" and detects errors based on makhraj (articulation points) [[126](https://qariai.app/compare/qari-ai-vs-tarteel)]. This suggests a different architectural choice, possibly one that moves beyond traditional ASR to incorporate direct classification of articulatory features. Such a system might use acoustic features extracted from short audio frames (like MFCCs) and feed them into a neural network trained to classify the makhraj of the currently spoken letter [[26](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/272850254_Makhraj_Recognition_for_Al-Quran_Recitation_using_MFCC), [95](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356874014_Mispronunciation_Detection_in_Articulation_Points_of_Arabic_Letters_using_Machine_Learning)]. While the provided sources do not offer a deep technical breakdown of Qari AI's model, its marketing emphasizes a lower-level, more anatomical understanding of pronunciation error, contrasting with the higher-level word-sequence approach typical of ASR. This presents an alternative research direction for future exploration, moving away from a phoneme-decoding-first approach toward a direct makhraj-classification model.

Other apps like **Tajweed Checker** and **Quran Companion** also advertise AI-powered Tajweed analysis and real-time voice recognition, indicating a competitive landscape where this technology is becoming a standard feature [[135](https://tarteel.ai/blog/top-5-quran-apps-for-memorization-on-ios-and-android/), [145](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=uqa.tajweedchecker)]. These apps often highlight rule-level correction, suggesting they possess some form of internal logic to identify which Tajweed rules were applied correctly or incorrectly [[146](https://qariai.app/academy/ai-tajweed)]. The existence of these products validates the market demand and confirms that the technological underpinnings are mature enough for commercial deployment. However, none of these companies publish the detailed technical specifications or model weights required for a direct, off-the-shelf implementation. Therefore, while they provide confidence that the goal is achievable and offer insights into potential architectures, they do not replace the need for developing a custom solution based on open-source components. Their success story, however, serves as a strong motivator and a proof of concept that a well-engineered system can deliver tangible educational value [[98](https://ejournal.um.edu.my/index.php/quranica/article/view/64936)].

## Architectural Pathways to a Custom Rule-Based System

The most promising and realistic pathway to achieving the user's goal—a low-false-positive Tajweed error detector—is a modular architecture comprising three distinct stages: a continuous-speech ASR model, a high-fidelity forced alignment tool, and a custom-built rule engine. This design philosophy directly addresses the shortcomings of monolithic models and leverages the available open-source ecosystem to construct a flexible, powerful, and controllable system. The user's intention to own and build the rule engine is a key strength, as it ensures the final product is perfectly aligned with a specific pedagogical curriculum, a level of customization that is unlikely to be found in any off-the-shelf solution. This section outlines a concrete, actionable pipeline based on the preceding analysis.

The first stage of the pipeline is the **Continuous-Speech ASR Model**. The foundation for this component should be a powerful, open-weight model like OpenAI's Whisper. As established, several versions have already been fine-tuned for Arabic Quran recitation, providing a strong starting point [[50](https://huggingface.co/tarteel-ai/whisper-base-ar-quran), [84](https://huggingface.co/Habib-HF/tarbiyah-ai-v1-1), [143](https://huggingface.co/yousifgamalo/quran-s-finetuned)]. The recommended action is to select one of these models (e.g., `tarteel-ai/whisper-base-ar-quran`) and perform further fine-tuning on a large corpus of continuous Hafs 'an Asim recitations, such as the Tadabur dataset [[88](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.18932)]. This second stage of fine-tuning is crucial for adapting the model to the specific prosodic patterns, coarticulation effects, and melismatic styles inherent in Qur'anic recitation, thereby mitigating the failure mode observed with isolated-word models [[90](https://huggingface.co/TBOGamer22/wav2vec2-quran-phonetics)]. The output of this stage is a transcript of the continuous audio, serving as the textual input for the next stage.

The second stage is **High-Fidelity Forced Alignment**. With the transcript from the ASR model and the original audio file, the Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA) is the ideal tool for generating precise time boundaries for each word and, subsequently, each phoneme [[228](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.18466v1)]. To ensure the highest possible accuracy, the MFA should be configured to use a custom pronunciation dictionary that includes Tajweed-specific phonemes or symbols [[226](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.19363v1)]. This prevents the aligner from breaking down complex Tajweed sounds into non-existent or incorrect sequences of standard phonemes. The result of this stage is a detailed, time-stamped transcription, where every phonetic segment is accurately mapped to its corresponding point in the audio stream. This alignment data is the most critical intermediate output, as its precision directly determines the reliability of the final error detection.

The third and final stage is the **Custom Rule Engine**. This is the user-owned component where the Tajweed analysis occurs. Taking the time-aligned Tajweed-aware phonemes from the forced alignment stage, the rule engine can now systematically check for violations of specific Tajweed rules. For example:
*   **Al-Madd Rules:** The engine can measure the duration of a vowel sound (harakah) following a medd letter and compare it against the expected number of harakats (e.g., 4, 5, or 6) for that specific type of Madd.
*   **Ghunnah Detection:** It can verify the presence and duration of nasal vibration (ghunnah) associated with a tanween or a shaddah on a noon or meem.
*   **Ikhfaa and Idghaam:** It can check for the appropriate voicing and nasalization when a saakin letter is pronounced after a noon or meem.
*   **Makhraj and Sifat Verification:** While not directly outputting makhraj/sifat, the phonetic script can be mapped to these concepts. The engine can cross-reference the phoneme with a lookup table defining its canonical makhraj and sifat. It could then flag instances where the acoustic properties of the audio segment (which could be analyzed separately) deviate significantly from the expected profile for that letter's articulation point.

This modular approach offers immense advantages. The ASR and alignment components can be continuously improved by incorporating new models and better datasets without affecting the rule engine. Conversely, the pedagogical rules themselves can be updated, expanded, or customized for different curricula without needing to retrain the entire system. This separation of concerns is the cornerstone of a robust, maintainable, and ultimately accurate Tajweed error detection system.

## Evaluation Metrics and Performance Benchmarks

Achieving the user's ultimate goal of "near-zero false positives on a verified-correct reciter" requires a rigorous and multi-faceted evaluation strategy. The failure of general-purpose audio LLMs highlights the danger of relying on high-level metrics or qualitative assessments alone [[68](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.18064)]. A quantitative, systematic approach is necessary to validate the performance of each component in the proposed pipeline and the system as a whole. The evaluation should focus on minimizing false positives on a diverse set of "gold standard" correct recitations while also maintaining sensitivity to actual errors.

For the ASR and forced alignment stages, the primary metric is **Word Error Rate (WER)** and **Phoneme Error Rate (PER)**, calculated by comparing the system's output transcript against a human-verified reference transcript [[55](https://www.protocols.io/view/precision-in-quranic-arabic-recitation-leveraging-drp355qn.pdf)]. However, for Tajweed analysis, a simple WER is insufficient because it treats all errors equally, whether they are grammatical, lexical, or phonetic. A more nuanced metric is the **Goodness of Pronunciation (GOP)** score, which measures the deviation of a speaker's pronunciation from that of a native speaker, typically by comparing acoustic features [[55](https://www.protocols.io/view/precision-in-quranic-arabic-recitation-leveraging-drp355qn.pdf), [222](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11977302/)]. While GOP is often used for general speech, its principles can be adapted. The evaluation should involve a held-out test set of continuous recitation audio from multiple canonical qaris (e.g., Al-Husary, Al-Afasi, As-Sudais) [[25](https://huggingface.co/datasets/zaibihassan/Quranic-Recitation-Data)]. The system's performance should be measured not just by its overall WER, but by its ability to correctly transcribe Tajweed-specific phonemes and its false positive rate on these correct samples. The goal is to achieve a WER as low as possible while keeping the false positive detection rate on known-correct audio below a very strict threshold (e.g., <1%).

For the custom rule engine, evaluation shifts from string matching to logical validation. The performance can be assessed using standard classification metrics: **Precision, Recall, and F1-Score** for each specific Tajweed rule (e.g., Al-Madd, Ghunnah, Ikhfaa). Precision is particularly critical here, as it directly measures the ratio of true Tajweed errors to all detected errors, which is the inverse of the false positive rate. A high-precision system minimizes alerts for correct pronunciations. The QDAT dataset provides a ready-made benchmark for this stage, allowing for the calculation of these metrics against expert-annotated correctness labels [[123](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06429), [161](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.23470v2)]. For instance, a study using QDAT reported an accuracy of 99.34% for Ghunnah detection, which translates to a very low error rate [[161](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.23470v2)]. The proposed pipeline aims to achieve similar or better performance, but across a wider range of Tajweed rules and in a continuous speech context.

The table below outlines a proposed evaluation framework for the end-to-end system.

| Evaluation Stage | Metric(s) | Gold Standard Data | Goal / Target |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **ASR Transcription** | Word Error Rate (WER), Phoneme Error Rate (PER) | Human-verified transcripts from datasets like Quran-MD [[136](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/133014)] | Minimize WER/PER; keep false positive transcription rate < 0.1% on correct audio |
| **Forced Alignment** | Frame-level Accuracy, Phone-level Boundary Error Rate | Time-aligned word/phoneme data from Quran-MD [[136](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/133014)] | Ensure > 98% alignment accuracy; minimize boundary errors that affect rule checks |
| **Rule Engine (Specific Rules)** | Precision, Recall, F1-Score per rule (Madd, Ghunnah, etc.) | Expert-annotated data from QDAT [[137](https://ijasat.com/index.php/ijasat_en/article/view/86)] | Achieve Precision > 95% for each rule to minimize false alarms |
| **End-to-End System** | False Positive Rate on Correct Recitations, True Positive Rate on Incorrect Recitations | A curated test set of correct recitations (multiple qaris) and a smaller set of intentionally flawed audio | Achieve near-zero false positives (~0%) on the correct recitation set; maximize true positives |

Ultimately, the most important benchmark will be the system's behavior on a diverse set of verified-correct recitations from canonical qaris. The user's own experience with the wav2vec2 model producing an 89% false-positive rate on a correct Al-Husary recitation is a stark reminder of the brittleness of models not designed for continuous speech [[90](https://huggingface.co/TBOGamer22/wav2vec2-quran-phonetics)]. The success of the proposed Whisper + MFA pipeline will be measured by its ability to avoid this pitfall. Through iterative testing, refinement of the fine-tuned ASR model, and careful calibration of the alignment parameters, it is plausible to develop a system that meets the demanding requirement of near-zero false positives, transforming it from a theoretical possibility into a practical reality.

## Strategic Synthesis and Feasibility Assessment

After a comprehensive analysis of the available models, datasets, and commercial approaches, a clear verdict emerges: while no single, off-the-shelf solution exists that perfectly satisfies all the user's stringent requirements, the construction of a highly effective, low-false-positive Tajweed error detection system is a feasible endeavor using a carefully assembled pipeline of open-weight components. The initial failures with general-purpose audio LLMs and isolated-word ASR models were not indicative of an insurmountable problem, but rather highlighted the necessity of a domain-specific, continuous-speech approach that bridges the gap between raw audio and articulatory-level analysis [[68](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.18064), [90](https://huggingface.co/TBOGamer22/wav2vec2-quran-phonetics)]. The current technological landscape provides all the necessary building blocks to construct such a system today.

The cornerstone of this effort is the identification of the **Quran-MD dataset**, which offers the rare combination of high-quality, time-aligned word and phoneme-level annotations for continuous Qur'anic recitation across multiple canonical qaris [[87](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.17880), [136](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/133014)]. This resource is indispensable for training and, most importantly, for rigorously evaluating the forced alignment component of the proposed pipeline. Paired with the vast scale of the **Tadabur dataset**, which provides over 1400 hours of naturalistic recitation, it becomes possible to train a robust acoustic model capable of handling the nuances of melismatic speech [[88](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.18932)]. The strategic selection and fine-tuning of a powerful base model like **Whisper**, as evidenced by existing community efforts, forms the first pillar of the architecture [[50](https://huggingface.co/tarteel-ai/whisper-base-ar-quran), [84](https://huggingface.co/Habib-HF/tarbiyah-ai-v1-1)].

The second pillar is the adoption of a modular, two-stage decoding process. First, a fine-tuned Whisper model generates a transcript of the continuous audio. Second, this transcript is fed into the **Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA)**, a proven and flexible tool, to produce precise time boundaries for each phoneme [[228](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.18466v1)]. This alignment is then enriched by passing the aligned words through a **Tajweed-aware phonemizer**, such as the one proposed by Hetchy or a custom implementation of the Quran Phonetic Script (QPS), to create a phonetic representation that implicitly encodes Tajweed rules [[8](https://huggingface.co/papers/2509.00094), [82](https://github.com/Hetchy/Quranic-Phonemizer)]. This phonetic stream is the critical output that feeds the final, user-owned component: the **custom rule engine**. This engine, built to the user's specific curriculum, performs the logical checks for Tajweed violations based on the time-aligned phonetic data.
